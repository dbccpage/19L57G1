# Seed — Oriented and Unoriented: one boundary, or two kinds?

`AuthorityEffect: None.` A derivation seed, to be run **blind** by an agent that
has not seen this discussion, in a fresh session, using only the sealed
repository. No external theory, vocabulary, or interpretation enters. The words
below are the object-language's own; do not import others, and do not target a
predetermined conclusion. Classify every notion; use *forced, minimal, free,
canonical, equivalent, universal, complete* only with a proof or a stated scope;
retain every tied minimum unless a proof eliminates it; state the strongest
defensible terminal result, including failure.

## The starting inscription

Admit two data and one **boundary**: a binary form over the two data, presented
with no other marks. A boundary is a whole with two sides; an instance of the
boundary places the two data across it. Nothing yet says whether the two sides
are interchangeable.

## The question, in the kernel's own terms

A boundary instance may be read two ways:

- **Unoriented.** The instance `(a, b)` and its reverse `(b, a)` are the same
  offering: the two sides are interchangeable, and no admitted structure tells
  one from the other.
- **Oriented.** The instance carries a **direction**: `(a, b)` and `(b, a)` are
  not the same offering, and something admitted witnesses which side is which.

Everything after is about whether that difference is real, what it costs, and
whether it is one object or two.

## Challenges

**C0 — the bare boundary.** Analyze the initial inscription with no
object-language operations. Prove exactly what the boundary alone separates and
what it cannot, before any orientation is chosen.

**C1 — the unoriented reading.** Give the boundary the unoriented reading: admit
whatever makes `(a, b)` and `(b, a)` indistinguishable to every admitted
observer. Prove that the two sides are internally interchangeable — that the
swap of the two data is an automorphism of what you admitted — and state what,
if anything, this reading separates.

**C2 — the oriented reading.** Give the boundary the oriented reading: admit
whatever makes `(a, b)` and `(b, a)` distinguishable to an admitted observer.
Prove that the swap of the two data is now **not** an automorphism, and exhibit
the observer (a context and its profile) that witnesses the direction. Count,
exactly, every capability this reading uses that the unoriented reading did not.

**C3 — same kind, or two kinds?** Decide, by the implemented isomorphism and the
declared separability — never by stipulation — whether the oriented boundary and
the unoriented boundary are the **same kind** (isomorphic, or observationally
equivalent) or **different kinds**. If different, name the exact invariant that
separates them. If the same, exhibit the isomorphism and show what happened to
the direction under it.

**C4 — where the direction lives.** Determine whether the direction of the
oriented reading is **internally witnessable** — visible to an observer built
from admitted material — or whether distinguishing `(a, b)` from `(b, a)`
requires a stance outside every admission (an observer that is never itself
admitted). If it is internal, give the admitted witness and its capability cost.
If it is external, prove that no admitted observer suffices, and classify that
external stance precisely (is it an admissible construction of the sealed
grammar, or would it be a new primitive?).

**C5 — one object, or two? (the collapse test).** Run it both ways.
1. **Start as two.** Admit an unoriented boundary and an oriented boundary as two
   separate objects. Ask whether the machinery **forces them together** — an
   isomorphism or an observational identification collapsing them to one — or
   keeps them apart. Prove which.
2. **Start as one.** Admit a single boundary and ask whether a genuine
   **distinction emerges** from admitted operations alone — whether "oriented"
   and "unoriented" can be told apart without positing them as two from the
   start. Prove which.
State whether the split between the two readings is a real difference in kind, a
single object read two ways, or a difference available only from outside every
admission.
