# Practice: new counts and new directions

Use the operations introduced in Lessons 00–07. Give a reason for each
answer; do not treat a familiar-looking number as evidence.

1. A table has counts `(3, 1)`. What is the total, and what is each
   position's share? Compare it with `(6, 2)` and `(3, 2)` by crossing
   each entry with the other table's total.
2. Compare `(3, 1)`, `(0, 0)`, and `(1, 3)` in a chain using the raw
   cross test. Which positive-total condition is needed for transitivity?
3. Find the separate squares and the add-then-square reading for
   `(3, 2)` and `(3, −2)`. Is the reading the sum of separate squares?
4. Hold the first coordinate pair at `(2, 0)`. Use `(0, 2)` and `(0, −2)`
   as the second pair. Compute both separate weight rows and readings.
   Does changing a preparation always change the reading?
5. Instead pair `(2, 0)` with `(2, 0)`, `(0, 2)`, or `(−2, 0)`.
   Which separate weights are shared? Which three readings occur?
6. On those three preparations, can the separate weights determine
   the combined reading? Give a pair of rows blocking a lookup. Does
   the answer change if the permitted carrier has only the first row?
7. With six loop positions and reversal-preserving readings, list
   the reversal groups. What is the greatest number of distinct readings
   allowed? Give a reading rule that attains it, without assuming the
   two-coordinate formula at this new loop size.
8. A proposed application gives the model's numbers exactly on a
   computer. What further connection would be needed before saying
   that the calculation predicts a physical measurement?

These are supplied teaching examples. Their finite calculations are
checked separately from the source's fixed three-preparation instance.
The [answers](ANSWERS.md) follow on a separate page.
