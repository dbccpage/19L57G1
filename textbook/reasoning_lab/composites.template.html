What reaches the other part?

Change one part of a supplied four-register system. Compare individual rows before comparing their outcome shares. Then inspect a graph built from operation labels.

These are supplied model records. No device observations or physical distances were measured.

1. Predict the change in B's outcome shares

A has registers A1/A2; B has B1/B2. “Change A2” adds one. A relay copies A2 into B1. For relay procedures, the baseline also runs the relay, with no A2 change. Every listed row has equal weight, including repeats.

How to calculate the outcome-share distance

For each possible mark, subtract its two shares and take the size of the difference. Add those sizes and divide by two. The result is called total variation distance. Zero means equal shares for this selected readout, not necessarily equal marks on each paired row. For example, changing [0,1] into [1,0] gives zero share distance even though both rows change.

2. Predict a distance in the declared graph

The rule list supplies undirected edges: every named read is joined to every different named write, and different writes in one operation are joined. Read/read pairs alone add no edge. Drawing this graph executes no operation. Its edges do not carry a direction or a speed.

What the labels alone cannot check

The Python write guard checks keys in a returned update dictionary. It does not inspect what a callback reads, check an observable's dependency label, or prevent a callback from mutating the original dictionary. These examples use explicit, nonmutating functions; a misleading declaration is retained as a contrast. Lean's local-change theorem instead uses functions whose arguments and output fields enforce the stated dependency.
