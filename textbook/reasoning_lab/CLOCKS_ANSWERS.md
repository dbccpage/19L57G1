# Clock and future-reading answers

Try the [practice](CLOCKS_PRACTICE.md) before reading this guide.

1. At update 3 the counts are 23 and 10; at update 8 they are 28
   and 13. The changes are 5 and 3, giving 5/3. The total ratio
   28/13 includes the initial offsets and answers a different question.
2. At update 2 they are 24 and 11; at update 5 they are 30 and 14.
   Changes 6 and 3 give ratio 2. That follows from the uniform rule,
   not the different pulse rule. At zero updates the dividing change
   is zero, so this ratio operation refuses an answer.
3. The first four lamp pairs are (0,0); the next is (1,0). It refutes
   promoting these four comparisons to agreement at every future
   index. It uses another readout/update: the native Lean theorem
   relies on A/B determining their own next values.
4. The complete states differ in clocks. Their A/B readouts remain
   equal at every index by the book’s theorem for this particular
   source transition. With B changed to 8, disagreement is already
   present at index 0: (5,7) versus (5,8).
5. It records index 0 as (1,3) and index 1 as (2,0). The state after
   two updates, (3,1), is not an additional recorded sample. Shifting
   a three-sample allowance retains new indices 0,1, corresponding to
   old 1,2. More would need another comparison or a justified property
   of the specific model, such as its all-future equivalence theorem.
6. For example, pulse intervals containing a positive even number
   of updates also give ratio 2, although their one-update sequences
   differ from the uniform rule. Or counters 20/9 become 30/14 after
   five uniform updates: elapsed ratio 2 does not force total ratio
   2. Retain actual start/end association, counter origins, tick meaning,
   interval choices, conditions, repeats and disagreement when testing
   a proposed clock model. These supplied examples measure no physical
   duration. Other reasoned answers can expose the future-claim premise.
