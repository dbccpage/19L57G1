# Worked practice answers

[Return to the questions](PRACTICE.md) · [First steps](README.md)

1. **heavy?** gives P and Q the pattern Yes, and R and S the pattern No.
   The groups are `{P, Q}` and `{R, S}`. Braces here mean “these items
   grouped together”; they are writing used by the reader.
2. Both questions give P and Q the pattern Yes, No; R gets No, Yes;
   S gets No, No. The groups are `{P, Q}`, `{R}`, `{S}`. P and Q still
   agree. R and S are separated by **sealed?**, whose answers are Yes
   and No. One added question need not separate every pair.
3. No group changes. The assumption is that the recorder reads these
   supplied test answers and has no label-reading question. The new
   name changes how we refer to P, while the table stays fixed.
4. R and S were separated by **sealed?** and become merged when only
   **heavy?** remains. The example refutes “every change preserves all
   old distinctions.” It does not refute the narrower result for adding
   a question while keeping all old answers unchanged.
5. Recovery fails. P and Q share the recorded pattern Yes, No but need
   urgent answers Yes and No. A rule receiving only that pattern cannot
   choose the right different answer in both cases.
6. Recovery succeeds on the declared list. Return Yes for Yes, No;
   return No for No, Yes; return Yes for No, No. Every item in a group
   has the group's assigned ordinary answer. The target table was
   supplied. This calculation supplies no measurement of actual parcels.

An investigation question could be: “Could two objects give the same
reading on this sensor while differing on the measurement my task needs?”
We would need to identify the proposed objects or conditions, measure the
sensor's actual responses and the target quantity, and investigate error
and repeatability. This question is a way to use the idea; its empirical
answer has not been supplied by the parcel exercise.
